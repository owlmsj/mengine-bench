The Painter

Piet Mondrian (1872–1944) was a Dutch painter who spent his career stripping art down to essentials — from landscapes to grids.

Along with Theo van Doesburg he founded De Stijl in the Netherlands in 1917, a movement that wanted to rebuild art, design and architecture from pure relation.

The Picture

Also listed as Composition II in Red, Blue, and Yellow, the 1930 oil on canvas is almost nothing and perfectly balanced: a large red square at the top right, a small blue square at the bottom left, a small yellow rectangle at the bottom right — divided by thick black lines on a white ground.

It hangs in the Kunsthaus Zürich.

Schematic of the composition — not the work itself.

De Stijl and Neoplasticism

De Stijl (“The Style”) was founded in the Netherlands in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian. Its theory, Neoplasticism, proposed a universal visual language stripped of personal gesture and representation.

Mondrian’s 1930 composition is that theory taken to its quietest, most exact conclusion.

The Principles

  • Only horizontal and vertical lines — no diagonals.
  • Only the three primary colours: red, blue and yellow.
  • Only the non-colours: white, grey and black.
  • Asymmetric balance: meaning lives in the relations between planes, not in images.

Why It Matters

The painting’s asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference point for modern graphic design and architecture.

Every grid poster, every swiss-style layout, every interface built from cards and rules descends from this restraint: proof that a red square, a blue square and a yellow rectangle can hold a composition in perfect tension.
