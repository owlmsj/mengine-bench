Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow

Piet Mondrian · 1930 · oil on canvas

In 1930, the Dutch painter Piet Mondrian reduced painting to its bare essentials — and created one of the most influential images of the twentieth century.

This page tells the short story of Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow: who made it, the movement it belongs to, and why a few coloured squares still matter.

The painter

Piet Mondrian (1872–1944) was a Dutch painter and, together with Theo van Doesburg, a founder of De Stijl.

The movement

De Stijl was founded in the Netherlands in 1917. Its style, Neoplasticism, allows only horizontal and vertical lines, the three primary colours red, blue and yellow, and the non-colours white, grey and black.

The picture

Painted in 1930 in oil on canvas: a large red square at the top right, a small blue square at the bottom left and a small yellow rectangle at the bottom right, divided by thick black lines on a white ground.

Where it lives

The work is held in the collection of the Kunsthaus Zürich. It is also listed as Composition II in Red, Blue, and Yellow.

Why it matters

Nothing here is symmetrical. The huge red square outweighs the small blue and yellow patches, yet the picture holds together — an asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks.

That balance became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture. Once you have seen it, you keep seeing it — in layouts, façades and grids that borrow its logic of planes, lines and primaries.
