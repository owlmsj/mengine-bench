A study in primary colour

Composition with red, blue and yellow

Piet Mondrian reduced a painting to black lines, a white ground and three blocks of primary colour. The result, made in 1930, is a study in asymmetric balance and a lasting model for modern design.

01 The painter

Piet Mondrian, 1872-1944

A Dutch painter who spent his working life taking things out of painting.

He removed depth, shadow, diagonal and curve until only line, plane and colour were left. By 1930 the method was steady enough to produce this canvas: three coloured blocks held by a black grid on white.

02 The painting

One canvas, three colour blocks

Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow is oil on canvas, made in 1930 and held by the Kunsthaus Zurich. It is also listed as Composition II in Red, Blue, and Yellow.

The structure is easy to read once you stop looking for a subject. A white ground is cut by thick black lines. The colour is concentrated in three places.

  • A large red square sits at the top right.
  • A small blue square sits at the bottom left.
  • A small yellow rectangle sits at the bottom right.
  • Thick black lines divide the white ground.
03 The movement

De Stijl and Neoplasticism

De Stijl was founded in the Netherlands in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian. Its painting programme, Neoplasticism, allowed only the simplest elements.

No diagonals and no curves. Only horizontal and vertical lines. Only red, blue and yellow among the colours, with white, grey and black as non-colours. The restrictions were the method.

01

Straight lines only

Horizontal and vertical. No diagonals, no curves.

02

Three primary colours

Red, blue and yellow carry the colour.

03

Non-colours

White, grey and black hold the structure.

04 Why it matters

Balance without symmetry

Its asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture.

Nothing in the composition is centred. The weight is carried by unequal blocks and uneven spacing, and it still reads as settled. That is the lesson designers and architects took from it: balance can be built without symmetry.

Graphic design

Posters, grids and identity systems borrowed the economy of line and primary colour.

Architecture

Plans and facades took the same idea of proportion held in visible balance.
