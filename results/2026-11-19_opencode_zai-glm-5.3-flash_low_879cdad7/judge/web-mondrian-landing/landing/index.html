The Painting

In 1930, the Dutch painter Piet Mondrian reduced painting to its bare essentials: a large red square at the top right, a small blue square at the bottom left and a small yellow rectangle at the bottom right, divided by thick black lines on a white ground.

That is the whole picture — oil on canvas, today held at the Kunsthaus Zurich. Yet within those few elements lies its entire point: an asymmetric balance, where one dominant red field is held in equilibrium by two small accents at the opposite corners.

The Movement

The painting belongs to De Stijl, a movement founded in the Netherlands in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian. Its visual system, known as Neoplasticism, allowed only horizontal and vertical lines, the three primary colours red, blue and yellow, and the non-colours white, grey and black.

"Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow" is that rule book made visible — every element on the canvas obeys it.

Why It Matters

Its asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture. Once you have seen it, you keep seeing its grammar — in layouts, façades and grids — long after 1930.
