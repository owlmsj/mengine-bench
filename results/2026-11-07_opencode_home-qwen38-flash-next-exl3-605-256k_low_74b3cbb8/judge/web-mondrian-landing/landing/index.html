The painter

Piet Mondrian (1872–1944) was a Dutch painter. In 1917, in the Netherlands, he and Theo van Doesburg founded De Stijl — the movement that gave this painting its language.

Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow (also listed as Composition II in Red, Blue, and Yellow) belongs to that language at its most distilled: painted in 1930, in oil on canvas, today in the collection of the Kunsthaus Zurich.

The rules of the game

De Stijl's doctrine, which Mondrian called Neoplasticism, allowed almost nothing:

  • Only linesHorizontal and vertical — nothing diagonal.
  • Three coloursRed, blue and yellow — the primaries.
  • Three non-coloursWhite, grey and black.

The picture

Look again at the grid above. A large red square holds the top right. A small blue square sits at the bottom left; a small yellow rectangle answers it at the bottom right. Thick black lines divide the fields on a white ground.

Nothing is centred, nothing is symmetrical — and yet the composition stands perfectly still. That balance is the whole subject of the painting.

Its asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture.
