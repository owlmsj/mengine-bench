Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow

Piet Mondrian1930 · oil on canvas

The painter

Piet Mondrian (1872–1944) was a Dutch painter. With Theo van Doesburg he founded De Stijl in the Netherlands in 1917, and its visual doctrine — Neoplasticism — allowed only horizontal and vertical lines, the three primary colours red, blue and yellow, and the non-colours white, grey and black. This painting is that doctrine carried through completely.

The movement

De Stijl, founded in 1917, reduced painting to its barest grammar: right angles, primaries, and nothing else. No curve, no gradient, no anecdote — only the relations between line and colour.

Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow (1930) is one of the clearest statements of those principles. It is oil on canvas and is held in the collection of the Kunsthaus Zurich.

The picture

A large red square sits at the top right. A small blue square sits at the bottom left, and a small yellow rectangle at the bottom right. Thick black lines divide them on a white ground. Nothing is centred and nothing mirrors — yet the whole holds.

Why it matters

Its asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture. The idea that a layout could be built from unequal parts — one dominant mass, two small counterweights — outlived the canvas and entered the grids of posters, buildings and screens, including this page.
