01 — The painter

Piet Mondrian

Piet Mondrian (1872–1944) was a Dutch painter and, together with Theo van Doesburg, a founder of De Stijl in the Netherlands in 1917. His mature work stripped painting down to what he held essential: horizontal and vertical lines, the three primary colours, and the non-colours white, grey and black. This program is known as Neoplasticism.

02 — The rules

Only what is allowed

Neoplasticism permits almost nothing — and that is the point. Every element of this canvas is one of a fixed, tiny vocabulary:

Only horizontal and vertical linesNo diagonals, no curves, no perspective.
RedOne of the three permitted primary colours.
BluePlaced small, at the bottom left.
YellowPlaced small, at the bottom right.
White, grey and blackThe non-colours. Here: white ground, thick black lines.
03 — The picture

What you are looking at

A large red square at the top right. A small blue square at the bottom left. A small yellow rectangle at the bottom right. Divided by thick black lines on a white ground. Nothing else — and yet nothing is centred, nothing is symmetrical.

04 — Why it matters

Asymmetric balance

The painting holds itself together without symmetry: one large red mass is answered by two small blocks of blue and yellow, on unequal sides of unequal black lines. That asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture. When you meet a poster, a chair or a building organised in flat primaries and black lines, you are looking at a descendant of this canvas.
