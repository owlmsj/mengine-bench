De Stijl · 1930 · oil on canvas

Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow

One large red square, one small blue square, one small yellow rectangle, thick black lines on a white ground. In 1930 Piet Mondrian reduced a painting to that — and the reduction became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture.

Painter Piet Mondrian (1872–1944), Dutch
Year 1930
Movement De Stijl
Collection Kunsthaus Zurich

The picture

The work is also listed as Composition II in Red, Blue, and Yellow. Nothing in it is a picture of something else: the elements are the picture. A large red square holds the top right; a small blue square sits at the bottom left; a small yellow rectangle closes the bottom right. Thick black lines do the dividing, and white ground holds the whole thing open.

Balance here is not symmetry. The masses differ in size and position, and the painting stays upright on that asymmetry rather than on mirroring.

The movement

De Stijl was founded in the Netherlands in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian. This 1930 canvas is the movement’s programme carried out to the letter.

The underlying idea has a name: Neoplasticism. Its rules are restrictive on purpose — only horizontal and vertical lines, only the three primary colours red, blue and yellow, and the non-colours white, grey and black.

Neoplasticism, three rules

Only horizontal and vertical lines

No diagonals, no curves. The dividing lines meet at right angles.

Only the three primary colours

Red, blue and yellow — and no mixed or secondary hue.

Plus the non-colours

White, grey and black: the ground and the lines that hold the colours apart.

Why it matters

Its asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture.

That is the afterlife of the canvas: a way of organizing a surface — unequal areas, primary colour, black division, white space — that left the studio and turned into a general layout language.
