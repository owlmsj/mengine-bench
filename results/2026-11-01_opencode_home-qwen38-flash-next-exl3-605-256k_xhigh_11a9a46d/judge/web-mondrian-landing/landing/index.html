Piet Mondrian · 1930 · De Stijl

Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow

A large red square, a small blue square, a small yellow rectangle, divided by thick black lines on a white ground. In 1930, Piet Mondrian reduced a painting to nothing but lines and primary colours — and modern design never looked back.

See the facts

The painter

Piet Mondrian (1872–1944) was a Dutch painter who moved step by step from landscapes to pure abstraction, until a picture was only relationships between lines, planes and colours.

The year

1930

Oil on canvas. The work belongs to the collection of the Kunsthaus Zurich. It is also listed as Composition II in Red, Blue, and Yellow.

The picture

A large red square at the top right, a small blue square at the bottom left, a small yellow rectangle at the bottom right — thick black lines on a white ground.

The movement: De Stijl

De Stijl was founded in the Netherlands in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian. The group sought a universal visual language of pure abstraction and simplicity — painting, architecture and design stripped down to their essentials, in search of a new harmony after the old world had collapsed.

Neoplasticism, in four rules

Mondrian called his painting Neoplasticism. Its rules are the ones you can see in this canvas:

  • Only horizontal and vertical lines — no diagonals.
  • Only the three primary colours: red, blue and yellow.
  • Only the non-colours: white, grey and black.
  • Asymmetric balance: unequal planes held in tension, never mirrored.

Why it matters

Its asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture — you still see this grid in posters, interfaces and buildings today.
