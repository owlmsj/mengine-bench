The painter: Piet Mondrian

Piet Mondrian (1872–1944) was a Dutch painter who stripped painting down to its essentials. Over a decade he moved from landscapes to grids, until his canvases held nothing but straight lines and flat colour.

Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow (also listed as Composition II in Red, Blue, and Yellow), painted in 1930 in oil on canvas, is the distilled result — and it hangs today in the Kunsthaus Zurich.

1930

The picture

A large red square at the top right, a small blue square at the bottom left and a small yellow rectangle at the bottom right, divided by thick black lines on a white ground.

Nothing is centred, nothing is symmetrical — yet the unequal blocks hold each other in perfect tension. That asymmetric balance is the whole achievement of the canvas.

De
Stijl

The movement: De Stijl

De Stijl ("The Style") was founded in the Netherlands in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian. It sought a universal visual language for a new era — pure abstraction, pure order, in painting, architecture and design alike.

Mondrian's branch of it had a name: Neoplasticism. Its rules are the rules of this page:

Only straight lines

Only horizontal and vertical lines. No diagonals, no curves, no perspective.

Primary colours only

Red, blue and yellow — and nothing else on the palette.

Non-colours

White, grey and black for the ground and the structure of the lines.

Why
it
matters

Why it matters

Its asymmetric balance of a few primary-colour blocks became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture. Long after 1930, that grid keeps resurfacing: in posters, book covers, furniture, building facades — and, not incidentally, in the layout of websites like this one.

Mondrian proved that a handful of rectangles, honestly arranged, can carry more tension than a thousand details. That lesson is the reason a 1930 canvas still feels current.
