Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow

A large red square, a small blue square, a small yellow rectangle, a few thick black lines on white. With nothing else, Piet Mondrian balanced a whole canvas — and changed how the modern world looks at design.

The painter

Piet Mondrian

  • Born
    1872, the Netherlands

    1944
  • Known for
    Dutch painter, co-founder of De Stijl and pioneer of abstract art

Mondrian began as a landscape painter in the Dutch tradition, then stripped his work down decade by decade: first trees became grids, then grids became pure lines and colour. By 1930 he had arrived at the formula this painting perfects.

Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow (also listed as Composition II in Red, Blue, and Yellow) is oil on canvas and belongs to the collection of the Kunsthaus Zürich. It shows a large red square at the top right, a small blue square at the bottom left and a small yellow rectangle at the bottom right, divided by thick black lines on a white ground.

No curves. No shading. No perspective. Just the fewest possible elements, placed so precisely that the empty white feels as active as the red.

The movement

De Stijl & its principles

De Stijl was founded in the Netherlands in 1917 by Theo van Doesburg and Piet Mondrian. Its painters, architects and designers pursued a universal visual language they called Neoplasticism, built on strict rules:

  • Only horizontal and vertical lines — no diagonals, no curves.
  • Only the three primary colours — red, blue and yellow.
  • Only the non-colours — white, grey and black.

The rules were not a limitation but a belief: that beneath the chaos of the visible world lay a harmony of straight lines and pure colour, and that art could express it.

Why it matters

A reference for modern design

The painting's power is its asymmetric balance. The huge red square dominates, yet the small blue and yellow blocks hold it in equilibrium — tension resolved with nothing but placement. A few primary-colour blocks, arranged with total conviction, became a reference for modern graphic design and architecture.

You can trace its logic in magazine layouts, typography, furniture and building facades ever since. Whenever a designer reduces a page to grids, blocks and primary accents, they are speaking Mondrian's language.
